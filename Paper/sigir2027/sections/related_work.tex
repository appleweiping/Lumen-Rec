\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLM-based recommendation.}
LLMs are used as recommenders in two main forms. \emph{Discriminative} recommenders pose a binary question about
a user--item pair and score the item by the probability of answering \emph{Yes}; TALLRec~\citep{bao2023tallrec}
instruction-tunes an LLM on such questions, CoLLM~\citep{zhang2025collm} and BinLLM~\citep{zhang2024binllm}
inject collaborative signals into the same yes/no formulation, and LLaRA~\citep{liao2024llara} aligns sequential
representations with the language space. These works evaluate \py{} only as a discriminative score (AUC or
per-user UAUC); none reports its calibration, and none asks what the probability is made of. \emph{Generative}
recommenders produce item identifiers or titles directly~\citep{geng2022p5,bao2025bigrec}, with decoding
corrections~\citep{bao2024d3} and preference-optimisation objectives~\citep{chen2024sdpo}; SPRec and
Flower show that SFT and DPO amplify popularity bias in such models~\citep{gao2025sprec,gao2025flower}. We build
on the discriminative formulation because its output is an explicit probability, and we revisit the same-candidate
next-item protocol and baseline set of our earlier controlled study.

\paragraph{Uncertainty in LLM recommenders.}
UQRec~\citep{kweon2025uqrec} quantifies the predictive uncertainty of list-wise LLM recommendation from a
single inference, decomposes it into recommendation and prompt uncertainty, and uses it to choose prompts.
UGR~\citep{fan2026ugr} trains generative recommenders with an uncertainty-weighted reward that penalises
confident errors and uses confidence tokens for request rejection and list truncation. EviRank~\citep{yan2026evirank}
estimates position-level confidence for LLM ranking lists. Ravikumar~\citep{ravikumar2026hallucinating} audits
the verbalized confidence of zero-shot LLM recommenders against catalog membership, stratified by item
popularity. KnowSA~\citep{lee2026knowsa} estimates what an LLM knows about each item and augments prompts only
for low-knowledge items, finding popularity a poor proxy for that knowledge. Our work differs in its object and
its question: we analyse token-level yes/no confidence of zero-shot \emph{and fine-tuned} personalised recommenders
against labels that match the question, decompose it into an item term and a personal-evidence term, and use the
decomposition to change rankings.

\paragraph{Confidence and calibration of LLMs.}
Black-box confidence elicitation shows that verbalized confidence of LLMs is concentrated at high values and
only weakly separates correct from incorrect answers~\citep{xiong2024llmuncertainty}; LLMs nevertheless ``mostly
know what they know'' when asked to evaluate their own answers~\citep{kadavath2022language}, and sampling-based
measures such as semantic entropy detect confabulations~\citep{farquhar2024semantic}. ConfTuner trains verbal
confidence with a tokenized Brier score~\citep{li2025conftuner}. Proximity-informed calibration shows that
samples in low-density regions are more over-confident at matched confidence and introduces a matched-confidence
bias index~\citep{xiong2023procal}; we adopt that index for item popularity. For binary judgments, LLMs are
miscalibrated and less reliable on low-frequency entities~\citep{li2025mkj}. Calibration methods for
classifiers---temperature scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration} and binning~\citep{naeini2015ece}---are
monotone and therefore cannot reorder a single user's candidates, a point we make explicit in
Section~\ref{sec:prelim}.

\paragraph{Yes/no bias and prompt symmetrization.}
Language models' binary answers carry prompt- and label-dependent biases. Contextual calibration subtracts the
prediction for a content-free input~\citep{zhao2021calibrate}, domain-conditional PMI subtracts the prior of the
answer surface form~\citep{holtzman2021surface}, and batch calibration estimates the bias from a batch of
inputs~\citep{zhou2024batch}. Contrast-consistent search scores a statement by combining the probability of the
statement and of its negation~\citep{burns2023ccs}, and crossed symmetrization models framing artifacts that a
mirrored prompt cancels~\citep{huang2026yesno}. Recent studies find the yes/no bias of LLMs largely
global~\citep{braun2025acquiescence,zhang2025yesharder}. \method{} uses the mirrored-question estimator; what is new is the
finding that, in personalised recommendation, the cancelled artifact is item-specific and popularity-graded and
that cancelling it changes within-user rankings beyond what item-prior subtraction achieves.

\paragraph{Popularity, exposure, and calibration in recommendation.}
Popularity bias~\citep{abdollahpouri2017controlling} and its causal treatments~\citep{wei2021macr,zhang2021pda}
are long-standing concerns, as are feedback loops that homogenise exposure~\citep{chaney2018feedback,mansoury2020feedback}.
Calibrated probabilities for recommendation must account for missing-not-at-random feedback~\citep{kweon2022calibrated},
which fully observed data such as KuaiRec~\citep{gao2022kuairec} makes testable; calibrated probabilities also
support per-user list sizes~\citep{kweon2024perk}. Data selection for LLM-recommender fine-tuning~\citep{lin2024dealrec,mei2025goracs}
and denoising of implicit feedback~\citep{wang2021tce} motivate our appendix study of confidence-based pruning.
