\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as \emph{pointwise} recommenders. Given a user's history and a
candidate item, the model is asked ``Will this user like this item? Answer Yes or No,'' and the probability of
\emph{Yes} is the ranking score~\citep{bao2023tallrec,zhang2025collm,zhang2024binllm}. The same number is read as the
model's \emph{confidence}, and decisions rest on that reading: a system may abstain or truncate a list when confidence is
low~\citep{fan2026ugr,kweon2024perk}; what it shows feeds back into what it learns, a loop known to amplify popularity
bias~\citep{chaney2018feedback,mansoury2020feedback}; and fine-tuning data can be pruned by uncertainty~\citep{wu2025unit}
or by influence and coreset scores~\citep{lin2024dealrec,mei2025goracs}.

Whether the confidence deserves this reading is largely untested for the yes/no family, which is evaluated by
discrimination (AUC, per-user AUC or UAUC) and, to our knowledge, not by calibration~\citep{bao2023tallrec,zhang2025collm,zhang2024binllm}.
Work on uncertainty in LLM recommenders addresses list-wise rankings~\citep{kweon2025uqrec,yan2026evirank}, generative
recommenders trained with confidence-aware rewards~\citep{fan2026ugr}, or the verbalized confidence of zero-shot models
against catalogue membership~\citep{ravikumar2026hallucinating}. Three things are missing for the yes/no family.
\emph{Labels that match the question:} a ``like'' probability is easily scored against whether the candidate was the
user's next interaction, an event with base rate $1/101$ on the usual same-candidate panels (one positive, 100 sampled
negatives).
\emph{Disaggregation:} a ranking depends only on the order within one user's candidates and a serving decision on the order
across users, whereas calibration summaries pool both.
\emph{Reference points:} a prior-only item mean (other users' earlier ratings of the item), popularity and matrix
factorisation scored on the same pairs, without which a confidence number cannot be placed.
% MISSING_REF (optional support for the last sentence): Ferrari Dacrema et al., RecSys 2019, weak-baseline critique; see paper_missing_refs.txt

What remains open is how much of $\py$ is a prior about the item and how much is evidence about this user. Write the
log-odds of \emph{Yes} as $\ell(u,i)=b_u+\pi(i)+e(u,i)$: a user offset $b_u$, an item prior $\pi(i)$ that the model brings
to item $i$ whoever asks (possibly linked to popularity), and personal evidence $e(u,i)$. Only $e$
personalises the ranking, and the prior is what per-user calibration cannot touch: a short invariance lemma
(Section~\ref{sec:prelim}) shows that any strictly increasing per-user map of the scores, such as temperature or Platt
scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration}, leaves per-user AUC and within-user NDCG unchanged. Post-hoc calibration of this kind
therefore cannot improve ranking; what remains are item-dependent corrections, cross-user decisions and training.

We therefore audit the confidence of yes/no recommenders. Six questions that practitioners ask of a recommender that
reports confidence, S1--S6, become the research questions, each with a registered endpoint
(Table~\ref{tab:rq}).
\begin{list}{}{\introlistsetup}
\item[RQ1] Is the model sure when it is right?
\item[RQ2] When it is wrong, was it unsure, or confidently wrong?
\item[RQ3] Does serving the most confident items over-expose head items relative to the candidate pool (static, on
  next-item panels)?
\item[RQ4] How well does confidence track labels that match the question, and how much of it is item prior rather than
  personal evidence?
\item[RQ5] Is confidence higher for popular items, and is that warranted once item quality is controlled?
\item[RQ6] Can uncertainty flag noisy training examples, so that pruning them beats matched random pruning?
\end{list}

\paragraph{A registered audit.}
The panels, endpoints, thresholds and a claim-admission rule were registered before confirmatory data were scored: a finding
is claimed in the abstract only if it is confirmed on users untouched during design and has the same sign on at least one
Amazon domain and on a second backbone, and until then its wording stays direction-free. Not every registered gate was met. Under the
registered zero-shot prompt, a pilot on 1,500 ML-1M users reached UAUC 0.587 against a registered adequacy bar of 0.60,
while the next-item component of the same gate was met (NDCG@10 0.2093 against 0.1863 on 1,000 same-candidate sports
events); on a fully observed validity set (KuaiRec~\citep{gao2022kuairec}) the zero-shot UAUC was 0.469 against a registered
0.58, so exposure is analysed statically and no feedback loop is run. We report these as registered negative results of the
zero-shot channel under the registered prompt (Section~\ref{sec:exp-gates}). They motivated one registered prompt-remedy round,
written after them and before any further run: seven prompt variants, selection on the pilot users, one confirmatory gate on
fresh users.

Because TALLRec-style recommenders are fine-tuned~\citep{bao2023tallrec,zhang2025collm}, the LoRA regime carries the main
results if it meets its own registered gate, a mean UAUC of at least 0.65 over three seeds.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
% MISSING_REF (dataset citations at the first mention of ML-1M and Amazon in C1; already listed by [results] in paper_missing_refs.txt)
\begin{list}{}{\introlistsetup}
\item[C1] \emph{Protocol.} A pre-registered audit. On rated panels with labels that match the question (ML-1M, Amazon Toys,
  Video Games, Sports), Qwen3-8B is audited zero-shot and with LoRA (three seeds), with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct as replication
  backbone; on full-scale same-candidate next-item panels from an existing benchmark protocol (four domains $\times$ 10k
  users $\times$ 101 candidates), zero-shot. Every rated-panel table carries non-LLM reference rows; intervals are
  user-clustered, Holm correction applies within each question, and confirmation uses untouched users only.
\item[C2] \emph{Decomposition.} Estimators for the three parts of $\ell$: a personal share disattenuated by the split-half
  reliability of the prior, the information gain over the prior-only item mean, a star-permutation contrast (does the model
  use the ratings in the history at all?), a two-axis popularity analysis~\citep{xiong2023procal} partialled on item
  quality, and, in the fine-tuned regime, a pseudonym knockout with a real-brand placebo.
\item[C3] \emph{Actionability.} The invariance lemma, and a test of each route it leaves open against its control:
  item-dependent corrections (candidate-conditioned priors, and a two-view contrast~\citep{burns2023ccs} that we evaluate
  and do not propose) against a paraphrase placebo and a correlation-matched ensemble null; cross-user selective
  serving~\citep{geifman2017selective} against random coverage, and exposure at 10k-user scale against the pool base rate
  and against eight published recommenders on the same candidates~\citep{wang2024elmrec,wang2025irllrec,he2025llm2rec,liu2025llmemb,liu2024llmesr,zhang2026proex,zhang2026promax,ren2024rlmrec};
  and pruning against matched random pruning over five seeds.
\item[C4] \emph{A guide.} A one-table answer to S1--S6 (Table~\ref{tab:guide}), each tied to its registered endpoint.
\end{list}
